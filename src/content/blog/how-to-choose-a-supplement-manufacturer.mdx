Your manufacturer decides what goes inside the pack, so this is the most important choice you make when launching a supplement brand. A good one makes your launch smooth. A bad one can mean failed batches, delayed launches and customer complaints you can't fix with marketing.

Here is the checklist we use before recommending any manufacturer to a client.

## 1. Check the licences, not just the claims

Ask for copies and check that they are valid and cover your product category:

- **Nutraceuticals and health supplements:** the manufacturer's FSSAI licence, covering the category you want to make.
- **Ayurvedic products:** a manufacturing licence from the state Ayurveda licensing authority.
- **Cosmetics, skincare and haircare:** a valid cosmetic manufacturing licence.

Many factories list products they don't actually hold a licence for. If the paperwork doesn't match your product, move on.

## 2. Look for GMP, then go and see it

GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice) certification is a good sign, but a certificate on the wall is not the same as a clean, well-run factory. Visit if you can. Look at how raw materials are stored, whether the production area is clean and controlled, and whether they test incoming materials and finished batches.

## 3. Ask for samples and test reports

Before you commit, get samples of the exact formulation you will sell. Ask for a certificate of analysis (COA) for each batch, which shows what was tested and the results. Taste, texture, smell and mixability matter for protein powders and gummies; customers notice them immediately.

## 4. Understand MOQs and real costs

The minimum order quantity (MOQ) decides how much money you need upfront. Ask:

- What is the MOQ for my product, and for each flavour or variant?
- Is packaging included, or do I supply it?
- What does the price per unit include: raw material, filling, labelling, testing?
- How does the price change at two or three times the MOQ?

A low price per unit with a very high MOQ can lock up more cash than a slightly higher price with a small first batch.

## 5. Ask about lead times and reorders

Find out how long the first batch takes and how long reorders take. A brand that sells out and can't restock for eight weeks loses its momentum, and its marketplace rankings.

## 6. Agree on who owns the formulation

If you develop a custom formulation, agree in writing that it belongs to your brand and won't be sold to anyone else under another name. Put confidentiality, quality standards, batch testing and payment terms into a written agreement.

## The short version

Check licences against your product, visit the factory, test samples, understand the real cost of your first batch, and put everything in writing. It takes a few weeks, and it saves you from the most expensive mistakes a new brand can make.
